Why this, why me

I've visited rural villages in India more than once. The thing that stayed with me wasn't the poverty; it was how normal everyone treated it. A classroom of forty kids sharing six textbooks wasn't an emergency to them. It was Tuesday.

"She was still writing in the last quarter-inch of the last page of the only notebook she owned. She wasn't embarrassed. She was proud she'd made it last the whole year."
from my journal, Varanasi

This isn't bad luck. It's structural.

  • Supply, not access: schools exist and kids attend, but books, paper, and trained teachers don't reach every classroom.
  • Distance and cost: secondary schools are often miles away, pricing out families who need kids working or at home instead.
  • Compounding disadvantage: gender, caste, and state-level poverty stack on top of the base gap. It isn't evenly distributed.
  • 75 years of partial fixes: enrollment policy solved attendance. It never solved what happens once a child is sitting in the room.

What I keep coming back to

I can't fix a national education system. I can make sure two specific kids get one hour of real, focused attention every Saturday, and I can get a few hundred more notebooks into a few hundred more hands. That's small. It's also not nothing.

This project isn't about raising money. It's about showing up. Every week.

What this project actually does

Weekly virtual tutoring

Every Saturday, I teach math and English over video call to two children from my father's home village. Sessions are logged: what we covered, what clicked, what's still hard, so progress is real and trackable, not just a claim.

Supply drive and fundraiser

Funds raised go directly to Pratham and Room to Read, no middle step, no handling by me. Tracked publicly, tied to the concrete outcomes below.
